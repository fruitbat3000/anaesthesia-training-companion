## What the block is like

Labour ward is fast, busy and shared with midwives, obstetricians and neonatologists. You will site labour epidurals, give spinal anaesthesia for caesarean section, top up epidurals for theatre, and be part of the team for obstetric emergencies. Most of the work is urgent, much of it out of hours, and communication with the woman and her birth partner is central.

## What you need to show

The IACOA is a summative assessment, like the IAC. The RCoA's IACOA workbook (2022) sets it out:

- **When:** it usually starts after about a year of anaesthetic training, takes a minimum of 3 months, and must be completed by the end of 2 years of anaesthesia training.
- **What it allows:** joining the obstetric on-call rota. It does not complete the Stage 1 obstetric capabilities, so you will need more obstetric experience later in Stage 1.
- **The standard:** your obstetric trainers must judge that you can be trusted to do both EPAs at **supervision level 3** (a supervisor on call from home).

### EPA 3: pain relief in labour

Epidural analgesia in women with straightforward anatomy, recognising what makes insertion harder (such as high BMI) and telling a senior. Its key capabilities cover: explaining labour and the pain relief options; the risks, benefits and contraindications of each; assessing a woman before an epidural and escalating risk factors; inserting a lumbar epidural catheter; prescribing and following up epidural analgesia; troubleshooting; and knowing how to manage complications such as post-dural puncture headache.

### EPA 4: anaesthesia for obstetric operative procedures

Anaesthesia for ASA 1–2 women having uncomplicated urgent or emergency obstetric surgery, including category 1–3 caesarean section, trial of forceps, manual removal of placenta and perineal repair. It excludes ASA 3–4 patients, and you should start managing emergencies but get help early. Its key capabilities are grouped as:

- **Preoperative preparation:** assessing women for urgent surgery and recognising higher-risk features; the anatomy and physiology of pregnancy and labour; knowing your limits and calling for help.
- **Intraoperative care:** spinal anaesthesia and epidural top-up; general anaesthesia with rapid sequence induction; knowing how to manage a difficult airway after induction (in simulation), hypotension, a failed regional block and breakthrough pain.
- **Postoperative care:** pain, nausea and vomiting, fluids and VTE prophylaxis.
- **Emergencies and simulation:** general anaesthesia for caesarean section and the obstetric failed intubation drill (DAS/OAA) in simulation; knowing the management of maternal haemorrhage and maternal collapse.

## Signing off the IACOA

1. Record SLEs (A-CEX, DOPS, CBD, ALMAT) and link them to the key capability clusters: one for EPA 3, four for EPA 4. There is no minimum number; they should show progress towards level 3.
2. Add obstetric simulation as a personal activity and link it to the IACOA milestone. The simulation syllabus covers common obstetric emergencies (for example eclampsia, pre-eclampsia and haemorrhage), general anaesthesia for caesarean section and the failed intubation drill, rehearsed as a team.
3. Your Educational Supervisor starts a **Multiple Trainer Report**, completed by at least three consultants.
4. You start the HALOs for EPAs 3 and 4, link the MTR, and send them to your designated obstetric trainer.
5. You start the IACOA certificate, link the MTR and choose two obstetric trainers as signatories.

The decision is a holistic judgement by the obstetric faculty, using your LLP, the MTR, simulation, your logbook (especially spinals, epidurals and obstetric airway management) and what they have seen you do. Evidence can be used flexibly: those with previous obstetric experience may need less.

## Cases to seek out

- Labour epidurals and combined spinal–epidurals, including the difficult back and the inadequate block.
- Category 1–4 caesarean sections, converting epidural analgesia to anaesthesia.
- General anaesthesia for caesarean section (in simulation if not in practice).
- Postpartum haemorrhage, pre-eclampsia and the post-dural puncture headache review.

> [!tip] Make it count
> Ask for a DOPS on your first few epidurals and again once you feel confident: the change in supervision level is the evidence.
