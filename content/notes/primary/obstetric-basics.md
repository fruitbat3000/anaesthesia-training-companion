## Labour analgesia

- Options: non-pharmacological, **Entonox**, IM opioids (pethidine, diamorphine), **remifentanil PCA** (needs one-to-one midwifery, SpO~2~ monitoring and oxygen), and **epidural or combined spinal–epidural** (the most effective).
- Neuraxial analgesia: low-concentration local anaesthetic with opioid (e.g. levobupivacaine or bupivacaine 0.1% with fentanyl 2 µg/mL), given by programmed intermittent boluses or infusion with patient-controlled boluses. It does not increase the caesarean section rate; it may prolong the second stage and increase instrumental delivery.
- Contraindications and cautions as for neuraxial blocks (coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia — check platelets in pre-eclampsia, anticoagulant timing).

## Caesarean section

- **Categories of urgency:** 1 immediate threat to the life of the woman or fetus; 2 maternal or fetal compromise that is not immediately life-threatening; 3 no compromise but needs early delivery; 4 at a time to suit the woman and team. Category 1 is traditionally achieved within 30 minutes (a decision-to-delivery interval target, not a rule).
- **Spinal anaesthesia** is preferred (safer than GA): hyperbaric bupivacaine with intrathecal opioid (fentanyl and/or diamorphine), a **T4 block to touch** bilaterally before surgery, **phenylephrine infusion** to maintain blood pressure near baseline, left uterine displacement.
- **Epidural top-up** if a labour epidural is working well.
- **General anaesthesia** when regional is contraindicated or too slow: antacid prophylaxis, head-up pre-oxygenation, RSI (propofol or thiopental, rocuronium or suxamethonium), smaller tube, **follow the OAA/DAS obstetric difficult airway guideline** (including a plan to wake or proceed), volatile maintenance (keep ≥ 1 MAC until delivery then consider reducing to limit uterine relaxation), awareness risk.
- **Uterotonics:** oxytocin (a slow IV bolus, e.g. 3 units, and/or infusion — causes hypotension and tachycardia if given fast), then ergometrine (avoid in hypertension), carboprost (avoid in asthma), misoprostol.

## Pre-eclampsia

- New hypertension after 20 weeks with proteinuria or organ dysfunction (renal, liver, neurological, haematological, uteroplacental). **Eclampsia** = seizures.
- Management (NICE NG133): antihypertensives (labetalol, nifedipine, hydralazine), **magnesium sulphate** for severe pre-eclampsia and eclampsia (seizure prevention and treatment; watch reflexes, respiratory rate, urine output and levels; potentiates neuromuscular blockers), careful fluid restriction, check platelets and coagulation before neuraxial block, attenuate the hypertensive response to laryngoscopy if GA is needed (e.g. remifentanil, alfentanil or labetalol). Delivery is the definitive treatment.

## Major obstetric haemorrhage

- Causes: **4 Ts** — tone (atony, the commonest), trauma, tissue (retained placenta, abnormal placentation), thrombin (coagulopathy).
- **Call for help** (major obstetric haemorrhage protocol), large-bore access, warm fluids and blood, uterotonics, **tranexamic acid**, fibrinogen guided by viscoelastic testing or Clauss fibrinogen, cell salvage, surgical and radiological control, and a clear communication loop with the lab.

## Maternal cardiac arrest

Modified ALS: **manual left uterine displacement**, early airway management by the most experienced person, and **resuscitative hysterotomy (perimortem caesarean section)** within about **5 minutes** of arrest if gestation is over about 20 weeks (to improve maternal resuscitation). Consider the causes (haemorrhage, thromboembolism, amniotic fluid embolism, sepsis, pre-eclampsia, LAST, high block, anaphylaxis, cardiac disease). **MBRRACE-UK** reports describe the leading causes of maternal death.
