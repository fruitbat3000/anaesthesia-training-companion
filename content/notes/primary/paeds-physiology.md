## Airway

- **Large head and occiput** (neck flexion when supine: a shoulder roll helps in infants), **large tongue**, floppy U-shaped epiglottis, high **anterior larynx** (C3–4 in infants; C5–6 in adults).
- Traditionally the **cricoid** was described as the narrowest point; imaging suggests the glottis may be narrower in the sagittal plane, but the cricoid ring is the narrowest **complete, non-distensible** ring, so an oversized tube causes subglottic oedema. Resistance ∝ 1/r^4^: 1 mm of oedema greatly narrows a small airway.
- Short trachea (about 4–5 cm in neonates) → easy endobronchial intubation. **Obligate nasal breathers** for the first few months.

## Respiratory

- **Oxygen consumption about 6–8 mL/kg/min** (roughly double the adult value) with a small FRC → **rapid desaturation**.
- Horizontal ribs, compliant chest wall and fatigue-prone diaphragm (fewer type I fibres): increased work of breathing. Minute ventilation is driven by **rate** (tidal volume ~7 mL/kg is similar to adults).
- Closing capacity may exceed FRC in young infants.

## Cardiovascular

- Stroke volume is relatively fixed (stiff, less compliant ventricles): **cardiac output is rate-dependent**, and **bradycardia is dangerous** (often a sign of hypoxia).
- Dominant parasympathetic tone: vagal reflexes (laryngoscopy, suxamethonium, oculocardiac reflex).
- Blood volume: **neonate ~90 mL/kg, infant ~80 mL/kg, child ~70–75 mL/kg**.
- Hypotension is a **late** sign of hypovolaemia.

## Fluids, glucose and temperature

- Maintenance (the "4-2-1" rule): **4 mL/kg/h for the first 10 kg, 2 mL/kg/h for the next 10 kg, 1 mL/kg/h thereafter** (i.e. 100/50/20 mL/kg/day). Use **isotonic** fluids (NICE NG29); hypotonic fluids risk hyponatraemia because of ADH release.
- Neonates and infants have limited glycogen: risk of **hypoglycaemia** with prolonged fasting.
- High surface area to volume ratio, little subcutaneous fat, brown fat thermogenesis only → **rapid heat loss**.

## Drugs

- Larger ECF and total body water → larger volume of distribution for water-soluble drugs (e.g. suxamethonium dose is higher per kg in infants).
- Immature hepatic enzymes and renal function in neonates → reduced clearance of many drugs. MAC is highest in infants (about 1–6 months).
- **Weight estimates (APLS, ages 1–10):** (age + 4) × 2 kg is a common simple formula; use measured weight when possible.

## Useful formulae (starting points only)

- **Uncuffed tracheal tube internal diameter (mm):** age/4 + 4; **cuffed:** age/4 + 3.5 (cuffed tubes are now widely used).
- **Oral tube length (cm):** age/2 + 12; nasal: age/2 + 15.
- **Spinal cord** ends at about **L3 at birth**, reaching the adult level (L1–2) by about 1 year; the dural sac ends at about S3–4 in neonates (S1–2 in adults).
