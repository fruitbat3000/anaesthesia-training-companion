## Ionisation and pKa

- **pKa** is the pH at which a drug is 50% ionised.
- **Weak acids** are **more ionised when pH > pKa**; **weak bases** are **more ionised when pH < pKa**.
- Henderson–Hasselbalch: for a base, **log([BH^+^]/[B]) = pKa − pH**; for an acid, **log([A^−^]/[HA]) = pH − pKa**.
- **Only the un-ionised form crosses lipid membranes** readily.

**Clinical consequences**

- **Local anaesthetics** are weak bases (pKa about 7.6–8.1): the closer the pKa to tissue pH, the more un-ionised drug is available and the **faster the onset** (lidocaine pKa 7.9 is faster than bupivacaine 8.1). Infected (acidic) tissue increases ionisation and reduces effectiveness. Adding bicarbonate speeds onset.
- **Ion trapping:** a weak base crosses into a more acidic compartment, becomes ionised and is trapped (fetal acidosis increases fetal local anaesthetic and opioid levels; morphine concentrates in gastric acid).
- **Opioids:** alfentanil (pKa ~6.5) is about 90% un-ionised at pH 7.4 → faster onset than fentanyl (pKa ~8.4, <10% un-ionised), despite lower lipid solubility.
- **Thiopental** (pKa ~7.6) is a weak acid formulated at high pH as the sodium salt.

## Quaternary ammonium compounds

Permanently charged (N^+^ with four carbon groups) regardless of pH: **do not cross the blood–brain barrier or placenta** and are poorly absorbed orally (neuromuscular blockers, **glycopyrronium**, neostigmine). Compare **atropine** (tertiary amine: crosses the BBB → central effects).

## Protein binding

- Acidic drugs bind mainly to **albumin**; basic drugs to **α~1~-acid glycoprotein** (an acute phase protein, raised after surgery and trauma) as well as albumin.
- Only the **free fraction** is active and can be metabolised or filtered.
- Displacement matters for drugs that are highly bound (>90%) with a small Vd and low extraction (e.g. warfarin, phenytoin), especially if the free level cannot redistribute. Hypoalbuminaemia increases the free fraction of phenytoin.

## Isomerism

- **Structural isomers:** same formula, different structure (**isoflurane and enflurane**).
- **Stereoisomers:** same structure, different spatial arrangement. **Enantiomers** are mirror images with a chiral centre, designated R/S (absolute configuration) or +/− (optical rotation).
- A **racemic mixture** is 50:50. Enantiomers can differ in potency and toxicity:
  - **Levobupivacaine** (S) and **ropivacaine** (S) are less cardiotoxic than racemic bupivacaine.
  - **S-ketamine** (esketamine) is about twice as potent as R-ketamine.
  - **Cisatracurium** is one of ten atracurium stereoisomers: more potent, less histamine release.
- **Tautomerism:** dynamic structural isomers that change with the environment (**midazolam** has an open ring at pH < 4, water-soluble in the ampoule, and closes at physiological pH, becoming lipid-soluble). Strictly this is a pH-dependent ring opening with loss of water, not true tautomerism, but it is usually taught under this heading.
- **Geometric (cis–trans) isomers** arise around a double bond or ring: **mivacurium** is a mixture of cis–trans and trans–trans isomers (the cis–cis isomer is weak).
