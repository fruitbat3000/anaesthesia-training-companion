## Definitions

- **pH = −log~10~[H^+^].** Normal arterial pH 7.35–7.45, i.e. [H^+^] **35–45 nmol/L** (pH 7.4 = 40 nmol/L). A strong acid dissociates completely; a weak acid partially.
- **Buffers** resist changes in pH and work best within about one pH unit of their **pKa**.

## The bicarbonate buffer

- **Henderson–Hasselbalch:** **pH = 6.1 + log~10~([HCO~3~^−^] / (0.23 × PCO~2~ in kPa))** (0.03 × PCO~2~ if in mmHg).
- Although its pKa (6.1) is far from 7.4, it is the most important extracellular buffer because it is an **open system**: CO~2~ is regulated by the lungs and HCO~3~^−^ by the kidneys.
- Other buffers: **haemoglobin** (histidine imidazole groups; the most important non-bicarbonate buffer in blood), plasma proteins, phosphate (important intracellularly and in urine), bone.

- **Isohydric principle:** all the buffer pairs in a solution share the same [H^+^], so they are in equilibrium with one another; measuring one pair (bicarbonate) describes them all.
- **Renal handling:** the kidney reclaims filtered bicarbonate (proximal tubule, carbonic anhydrase) and excretes H^+^ bound to **phosphate** (titratable acid) and **ammonia** (as NH~4~^+^), generating new bicarbonate. The liver also contributes, through lactate metabolism and the balance between ureagenesis and glutamine production.

## Derived variables

- **Standard bicarbonate:** HCO~3~^−^ after equilibration at PCO~2~ 5.3 kPa and 37°C: removes the respiratory component.
- **Base excess:** the amount of strong acid (mmol/L) needed to return blood to pH 7.4 at PCO~2~ 5.3 kPa and 37°C. Normal −2 to +2; negative values indicate metabolic acidosis. **Standard base excess** is calculated for extracellular fluid (Hb about 50 g/L) and is more representative of the whole body.
- **Anion gap = (Na^+^ + K^+^) − (Cl^−^ + HCO~3~^−^)**, normally about 12–16 mmol/L with K^+^ (8–12 without). Unmeasured anions are mostly **albumin**: correct for low albumin (add about 2.5 mmol/L per 10 g/L fall).

## Four primary disorders and their compensation

| Disorder | Primary change | Compensation | Expected compensation (rules of thumb) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Metabolic acidosis | ↓ HCO~3~^−^ | Hyperventilation ↓ PCO~2~ | PaCO~2~ (mmHg) ≈ 1.5 × HCO~3~^−^ + 8 (± 2) (Winter's formula) |
| Metabolic alkalosis | ↑ HCO~3~^−^ | Hypoventilation ↑ PCO~2~ (limited by hypoxia) | |
| Respiratory acidosis | ↑ PCO~2~ | Renal HCO~3~^−^ retention (days) | Acute: HCO~3~^−^ ↑ ~1 per 10 mmHg; chronic: ~3.5–4 per 10 mmHg |
| Respiratory alkalosis | ↓ PCO~2~ | Renal HCO~3~^−^ excretion | Acute: ↓ ~2 per 10 mmHg; chronic: ↓ ~4–5 per 10 mmHg |

Compensation **does not overcorrect**; if the pH is on the "other side", suspect a mixed disorder.

## Metabolic acidosis: the anion gap

- **Raised gap:** lactate, ketones (diabetic, alcoholic, starvation), renal failure (sulphate, phosphate), toxins (methanol, ethylene glycol, salicylate).
- **Normal gap (hyperchloraemic):** large volumes of 0.9% saline, GI bicarbonate loss (diarrhoea, fistulae, ileal conduit), renal tubular acidosis, acetazolamide.

## The Stewart (physicochemical) approach in brief

pH is determined by three independent variables: **PCO~2~**, the **strong ion difference** (SID ≈ Na^+^ + K^+^ − Cl^−^ − lactate; normal about 40 mEq/L) and **total weak acids** (mainly albumin and phosphate). Bicarbonate is a dependent variable. It explains why 0.9% saline (SID 0) causes acidosis and why hypoalbuminaemia causes alkalosis.
