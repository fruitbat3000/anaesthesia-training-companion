## Consent

- Valid consent is **voluntary**, given by a person with **capacity**, who is **adequately informed**. It is a **process** (a dialogue), not a signature. Anaesthetists usually record consent in the anaesthetic record rather than on a separate form.
- **Material risks (Montgomery, 2015):** disclose risks that **a reasonable person in the patient's position** would want to know, or that **this particular patient** would attach significance to; discuss reasonable alternatives (including doing nothing). Use accessible numbers and patient information (e.g. RCoA leaflets).
- Consent can be withdrawn at any time. Emergencies: treat in the patient's best interests if they lack capacity and consent cannot wait.

## Capacity (Mental Capacity Act 2005, England and Wales)

- **Presume capacity**; do not treat someone as lacking capacity because they make an unwise decision; take all practicable steps to help them decide.
- **Two-stage test:** is there an impairment or disturbance of the mind or brain? If so, does it mean the person **cannot understand, retain, use or weigh** the relevant information, or **communicate** their decision?
- Capacity is **decision-specific and time-specific**.
- If a patient lacks capacity: act in their **best interests** (consider their past and present wishes, beliefs and values, and consult family and carers), choose the **least restrictive** option, and check for an advance decision to refuse treatment or a lasting power of attorney for health and welfare. An **IMCA** is appointed for serious decisions when there is no one else to consult. **Deprivation of Liberty** safeguards may apply.
- Patients with **learning disability, autism or dementia** need reasonable adjustments (e.g. hospital passports, familiar carers, quiet environments, premedication planning).

## Children and young people

Those with parental responsibility consent for young children. **Gillick-competent** children under 16 can consent for themselves. Young people of 16–17 are presumed to have capacity (MCA applies from 16). Refusal by a competent young person can in rare situations be overridden by the courts or those with parental responsibility — seek senior and legal advice.

## Safeguarding

- **Children:** be alert to signs of non-accidental injury (inconsistent history, delayed presentation, particular injury patterns); follow local child protection procedures — escalate to the safeguarding lead and do not investigate yourself.
- **Adults:** the principles are **empowerment, prevention, proportionality, protection, partnership and accountability**. Recognise abuse and neglect (physical, sexual, financial, psychological, neglect, domestic abuse, modern slavery) and refer.

## Professional duties

- **Good Medical Practice (GMC):** knowledge and skills, safety and quality, communication and partnership, and trust.
- **Duty of candour:** be open and honest with patients when something goes wrong, apologise, explain and support; organisations have a statutory duty of candour for notifiable safety incidents.
- **Raising concerns:** through the line manager, clinical governance, the Freedom to Speak Up Guardian, or the GMC; you are protected when raising concerns in good faith.
- **Governance and data:** incident reporting, audit, complaints, clinical guidelines; data protection (UK GDPR, Caldicott principles, confidentiality).
- **Employment law:** contracts, the Working Time Regulations (average 48-hour week with opt-out, rest requirements), rota rules; **equality and diversity** legislation (Equality Act 2010: protected characteristics).
