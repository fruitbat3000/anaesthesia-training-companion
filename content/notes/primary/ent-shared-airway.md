## The shared airway

The surgeon and anaesthetist work in the same space, so the airway is often out of reach, may be contaminated with blood and debris, and can be displaced by surgical manoeuvres. **Plan together**: device choice, tube fixation and position, throat packs (with a formal count and removal check), who controls the airway, and how you will manage problems.

## Devices

- **Reinforced (flexible) laryngeal masks** for many tonsillectomies, nasal and dental procedures (with surgical cooperation), **RAE preformed tubes** (south-facing oral for tonsillectomy with a mouth gag; north-facing nasal for dental and maxillofacial surgery), **reinforced tubes**, **nasal tubes**, microlaryngeal tubes, **laser-resistant tubes**, and tubeless techniques (jet ventilation, high-flow nasal oxygen) for laryngeal surgery.
- **Special surgical devices:** mouth gags (Boyle–Davis) can compress or kink tubes; lasers (fire risk: low FiO~2~, saline-filled cuffs); microdebriders; vasoconstrictors on nasal packs (cocaine or adrenaline: hypertension and arrhythmias).

## Common procedures

- **Tonsillectomy:** bleeding and airway protection; LMA or tube; multimodal analgesia (paracetamol, NSAIDs, dexamethasone, careful opioids — **no codeine** in children); PONV prophylaxis; smooth emergence in the lateral, head-down "tonsil position" or awake extubation.
- **Nasal surgery:** blood in the airway; a throat pack; emergence with the patient breathing through the mouth.
- **Dental extractions:** shared airway, throat packs, nasal tubes for longer procedures; many children and adults with learning disabilities (consent and capacity considerations).

## The bleeding tonsil

- Primary (within 24 hours) or secondary (days later, often infection-related). Dangers: **hypovolaemia** (often underestimated in children, who swallow blood), a **full stomach of blood**, and a difficult view.
- **Resuscitate first** (IV access, fluids and blood, group and crossmatch), senior anaesthetist and ENT surgeon, **RSI** with two suction devices and a smaller tube ready (or inhalational induction in the head-down lateral position in some cases), empty the stomach with an orogastric tube before extubation, and extubate awake in the lateral position.

## The obstructed airway

- Causes: tumours, infection (epiglottitis, abscess, Ludwig's angina), trauma, foreign body, haematoma, angioedema.
- Principles: oxygen, sit the patient up, heliox or nebulised adrenaline as temporising measures, senior anaesthetic and ENT help, imaging if time allows, and a plan with the surgeon for **awake tracheal intubation, awake tracheostomy (under local anaesthesia), or inhalational induction** in theatre with the surgeon scrubbed and ready for front-of-neck access. Avoid sedation and neuromuscular blockade before the airway is secured unless part of a planned strategy.
