## Extubation

- Extubation is an **elective** procedure: plan it (DAS extubation guidelines). **Low-risk:** fasted, uncomplicated airway, no general risk factors. **At-risk:** difficult airway at induction, airway surgery or oedema, restricted access, obesity/OSA, aspiration risk, cardiovascular or respiratory instability.
- **Preparation:** oxygenate (FiO~2~ 1.0), suction under direct vision, reverse neuromuscular block (TOF ratio ≥ 0.9), analgesia, position (head-up in most adults).
- **Awake extubation** (eyes open, obeying commands, adequate spontaneous ventilation) is standard for most at-risk patients. **Deep extubation** avoids coughing (e.g. after some ENT and eye surgery) but risks airway obstruction and aspiration; it requires experience.
- Advanced techniques for at-risk airways: **remifentanil extubation**, **SGA exchange** (Bailey manoeuvre), airway exchange catheters, postponing extubation or tracheostomy.

## Laryngospasm

- Reflex closure of the vocal cords, commonly in light planes of anaesthesia with airway stimulation (secretions, blood, suction, SGA removal), especially in children and smokers.
- **Signs:** stridor (partial) or silent obstruction (complete), paradoxical breathing, desaturation, bradycardia in children.
- **Management:** call for help, stop stimulation, **100% O~2~ with CPAP** via a tight-fitting mask, jaw thrust (and **Larson's point** pressure behind the ear lobe), deepen anaesthesia with **propofol** (e.g. 0.5–1 mg/kg in adults), and if it persists, **suxamethonium** (small doses, e.g. 0.1–0.5 mg/kg IV, or IM if no access in children) with atropine for bradycardia; intubate if needed. Watch for **negative-pressure pulmonary oedema**.

## Stridor after surgery

Causes: laryngospasm, laryngeal oedema (intubation, allergy), vocal cord palsy (recurrent laryngeal nerve after thyroid or neck surgery), haematoma (after thyroid/carotid surgery: **open the wound** — "SCOOP" the clips and sutures — then call for senior help), foreign body, residual block. Management includes oxygen, head-up, nebulised adrenaline, dexamethasone, heliox, and early senior airway expertise.

## Common problems in recovery

| Problem | Think of |
|---|---|
| **Hypoxaemia** | Airway obstruction, hypoventilation (opioids, residual block, sedation), atelectasis, bronchospasm, aspiration, pulmonary oedema, pneumothorax, PE, shivering (↑ O~2~ demand) |
| **Hypotension** | Hypovolaemia (bleeding), vasodilation (neuraxial, drugs), cardiac causes (ischaemia, arrhythmia), sepsis, anaphylaxis, tension pneumothorax |
| **Hypertension** | Pain, full bladder, hypercapnia, hypoxia, withdrawal of antihypertensives, emergence agitation, pre-eclampsia |
| **Confusion / delirium** | Hypoxia, hypercapnia, hypoglycaemia, hypotension, pain, full bladder, drugs (anticholinergics, benzodiazepines), sepsis, stroke, alcohol withdrawal, pre-existing cognitive impairment |
| Pain, **PONV**, shivering, urinary retention, hypothermia | See relevant notes |

Also recognise which patients need **higher-level care** (level 2 or 3) afterwards: major surgery, significant comorbidity, instability, ongoing organ support.

## Discharge from recovery (typical criteria)

Conscious and able to maintain their own airway, with protective reflexes; breathing adequately with acceptable SpO~2~ (on oxygen if prescribed); cardiovascularly stable with no unexplained changes; pain and PONV controlled; normothermic; surgical site checked; prescriptions and instructions for the ward completed. **Day-case discharge home** also requires a responsible adult, oral intake as appropriate, mobilisation, and written advice. Follow up patients on the ward after anaesthesia where possible.
