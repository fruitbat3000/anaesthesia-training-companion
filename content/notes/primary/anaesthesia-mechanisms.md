## Historical: lipid theories

- **Meyer–Overton (c. 1900):** potency correlates with lipid (olive oil) solubility across agents of very different structure, suggesting a common hydrophobic site.
- **Problems:** stereoisomers with the same lipid solubility have different potencies; some lipid-soluble compounds are not anaesthetic (non-immobilisers); the **cut-off effect** (potency stops increasing beyond a certain chain length); and pressure reversal is only partial.

## Current view: protein targets

| Target | Effect | Agents |
|---|---|---|
| **GABA~A~ receptor** (ligand-gated Cl^−^ channel) | Positive allosteric modulation → increased Cl^−^ entry, hyperpolarisation, inhibition | **Propofol, etomidate, thiopental**, benzodiazepines (hypnotic component), volatiles |
| **NMDA receptor** (glutamate) | Antagonism | **Ketamine, N~2~O, xenon** |
| **Two-pore domain K^+^ channels** (e.g. TREK-1) | Activation → hyperpolarisation | Volatiles, N~2~O, xenon |
| Glycine receptors (spinal cord) | Potentiation | Volatiles (immobility) |
| HCN channels, nicotinic receptors | Various | Volatiles |

## Where in the nervous system

- **Unconsciousness and amnesia:** cortex, thalamus (thalamocortical disconnection), hippocampus (amnesia at low concentrations), arousal systems in the brainstem and hypothalamus (propofol acts via GABA~A~ in the tuberomammillary nucleus).
- **Immobility** (the endpoint of MAC) is largely **spinal**: MAC is unchanged in animals when the brain is removed.

## Components of anaesthesia

Hypnosis, amnesia, analgesia (antinociception), immobility and autonomic stability. Different drugs contribute differently, which is why balanced anaesthesia combines them. **Accidental awareness** (NAP5: about 1 in 19,000 general anaesthetics reported, higher with neuromuscular block, TIVA errors, caesarean section and cardiac surgery) occurs when hypnosis is inadequate while the patient is paralysed.
