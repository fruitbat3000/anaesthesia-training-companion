## Before the block

- **Consent:** discuss benefits and specific risks (block failure, LAST, nerve injury — transient symptoms in a few per cent, permanent injury rare; block-specific risks such as phrenic palsy or pneumothorax). Document the discussion.
- **Assessment:** coagulation and anticoagulant timing (especially for deep or non-compressible blocks), infection, pre-existing neuropathy (document a baseline examination), and patient cooperation.

## Performing the block

**Stop Before You Block** (a moment immediately before needle insertion to confirm the site and side, often with the assistant; wrong-side blocks are "never events"), full monitoring, IV access, resuscitation equipment and lipid emulsion immediately available, asepsis, ultrasound with or without nerve stimulation, **injection pressure** monitoring, incremental injection and aspiration.

## Nerve injury

Mechanisms: needle trauma, intraneural injection (especially intrafascicular), toxicity of the local anaesthetic, ischaemia; plus surgical, positioning, tourniquet and patient factors. **Management:** examine and document, involve the surgeon, early neurology referral and nerve conduction studies for significant deficits, and follow-up.

## Regional anaesthesia in trauma patients

Benefits (analgesia, reduced opioids, delirium prevention, earlier mobilisation) versus concerns (masking **compartment syndrome** — rare; use lower concentrations, avoid dense motor block, communicate and monitor; coagulopathy; consent in acutely injured or confused patients).

## Quality and governance

Standardised documentation, block rooms and dedicated teams, training (the RA-UK and RCoA curriculum resources), audit of success, complications and patient satisfaction, and national registries.
