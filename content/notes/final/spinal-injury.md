## Acute injury

- **Assessment:** NICE NG41 criteria for imaging; **CT** is first-line in adults with suspected cervical injury; MRI for cord and ligament injury.
- **Airway:** manual in-line stabilisation (remove the front of the collar), video laryngoscopy, minimal neck movement; awake fibreoptic intubation in selected cooperative patients.
- **High cervical injuries** (above C3–5) impair diaphragmatic function → early ventilation; **lower cervical** injuries cause intercostal paralysis (vital capacity falls; monitor it and intubate early if falling).
- **Neurogenic shock** (injuries above about T6): loss of sympathetic tone → **hypotension with bradycardia** (warm peripheries); treat with fluids cautiously, vasopressors (noradrenaline), atropine. Maintain MAP (often 85–90 mmHg for several days in acute cord injury) to protect cord perfusion.
- **Spinal shock:** temporary loss of all reflexes below the injury (hours to weeks) — flaccid paralysis.
- **Suxamethonium:** safe in the first 24–48 hours; **avoid after about 72 hours** for many months (hyperkalaemia from receptor upregulation).
- Also: paralytic ileus, urinary retention, pressure areas, thromboembolism, hypothermia (poikilothermia), and the need for early specialist spinal unit involvement.

## Chronic spinal cord injury (from weeks after injury)

- **Autonomic dysreflexia** (lesions above about **T6**): a stimulus below the level (bladder distension, bowel, surgery, labour) triggers massive unopposed sympathetic discharge → **severe hypertension**, reflex bradycardia, sweating and flushing above the lesion, headache; risk of stroke and seizures. Prevent with adequate anaesthesia (GA, spinal) even for "painless" procedures below the level; treat by removing the stimulus, sitting up, and rapid-acting vasodilators.
- Respiratory impairment (weak cough, restrictive defect), spasticity and contractures (positioning), chronic pain, osteoporosis, recurrent UTIs, pressure ulcers, and hyperkalaemic response to suxamethonium.
