## Trauma systems and the team

Regional **major trauma networks** (major trauma centres and units), triage tools, pre-alerts. In the resuscitation room the anaesthetist usually leads the airway and often the resuscitation, within a team with a clear leader, defined roles, and closed-loop communication.

## Damage control resuscitation

- **Haemorrhage control** (tourniquets, pelvic binder, surgery, interventional radiology) is the priority.
- **Permissive hypotension** (e.g. systolic 80–90 mmHg, or a palpable radial pulse) in penetrating and blunt trauma **without traumatic brain injury**, until bleeding is controlled.
- **Blood-based resuscitation** with **early plasma and platelets** in balanced ratios (about 1:1:1 initially), minimal crystalloid, and **tranexamic acid** (1 g over 10 minutes then 1 g over 8 hours) **within 3 hours** of injury (CRASH-2). Then **goal-directed** replacement guided by laboratory and **viscoelastic** tests.
- **Prevent and treat the lethal triad** (hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy) plus **hypocalcaemia** (citrate).
- **Rapid infusion devices** with warming; large-bore access (including IO and central); cell salvage in selected cases.
- **Trauma-induced coagulopathy:** endogenous (shock and tissue injury activate protein C, hyperfibrinolysis, fibrinogen consumption) plus dilution, hypothermia and acidosis.

## Damage control surgery

Abbreviated surgery to stop bleeding and contamination, then **resuscitation in ICU** (warming, correction of coagulopathy), then definitive surgery. Beware abdominal compartment syndrome.

## Specific injuries

- **Chest:** tension pneumothorax (decompress), massive haemothorax (drain, surgery if > 1,500 mL initially or ongoing loss), flail chest and pulmonary contusion (analgesia — regional blocks such as erector spinae or epidural — and ventilatory support), cardiac tamponade, aortic injury. **Resuscitative thoracotomy** for traumatic arrest in selected cases (penetrating chest trauma).
- **Abdomen:** CT in stable patients; FAST; laparotomy for instability; splenic and liver injuries (embolisation).
- **Pelvis:** binder, interventional radiology or pre-peritoneal packing; beware urethral injury (catheter).
- **Limbs:** fractures, compartment syndrome (pain out of proportion; regional anaesthesia and monitoring — discuss with surgeons), fat embolism.

## Regional anaesthesia in trauma

Useful for rib fractures, hip and femoral fractures (fascia iliaca), and limb injuries; consider coagulopathy, compartment syndrome risk (communicate and document), and the need for neurological assessment.

## The metabolic response to trauma

Catabolism, hyperglycaemia, fluid retention; the ebb (shock) and flow (hypermetabolic) phases.
