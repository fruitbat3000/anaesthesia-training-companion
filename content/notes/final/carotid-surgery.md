## Indications and timing

Symptomatic carotid stenosis (TIA or minor stroke) with significant stenosis on the relevant side; surgery is most beneficial when performed **early (within about 2 weeks)** of symptoms.

## Goals

- **Maintain cerebral perfusion** (especially during clamping): keep blood pressure at or slightly above baseline; normocapnia (hypocapnia vasoconstricts; hypercapnia may cause steal).
- **Myocardial protection:** these patients have a high prevalence of coronary disease.
- **Rapid emergence** for neurological assessment; avoid coughing and hypertension.

## Regional versus general anaesthesia

- **Regional (awake):** **superficial and/or intermediate cervical plexus block** (the deep block is used less because of risks: phrenic nerve palsy, vertebral artery injection, intrathecal spread) plus surgical infiltration. Allows **direct neurological monitoring** of the awake patient during clamping (speech, contralateral hand grip), a guide to selective shunting. Needs a cooperative patient who can lie still; conversion to GA may be needed.
- **General:** a smooth induction, invasive BP, cerebral monitoring (stump pressure, near-infrared spectroscopy, transcranial Doppler, EEG) for shunt decisions.
- The **GALA trial** found no difference in stroke, MI or death between regional and general anaesthesia; choice is individualised.

## Complications

- **Stroke** (embolic or haemodynamic), MI.
- **Haemodynamic instability:** baroreceptor manipulation causes bradycardia and hypotension (local anaesthetic to the carotid sinus); denervation can cause labile BP postoperatively.
- **Neck haematoma:** can compress the airway rapidly — **open the wound** (remove sutures) and secure the airway early with experienced help.
- **Cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome** (days later): headache, seizures, intracerebral haemorrhage — strict blood pressure control.
- **Cranial nerve injuries:** hypoglossal, recurrent laryngeal, marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve.
