## Framing a question

**PICO**: Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome (± time). Choose outcomes that matter to patients (mortality, days alive and out of hospital, disability-free survival, quality of recovery) and define them precisely; recognise uncertainty in measuring them.

## Critical appraisal (key questions)

1. Is the question clear and important?
2. Is the design appropriate (RCT, cohort, case–control, systematic review)?
3. **Randomisation and allocation concealment** adequate? **Blinding**? Groups similar at baseline?
4. **Bias:** selection, performance, detection, attrition, reporting; **confounding** in observational studies.
5. **Sample size and power** (was the study powered for the primary outcome? a clinically important difference?)
6. Analysis: intention to treat; handling of missing data; appropriate tests; multiple comparisons.
7. **Results:** effect size with **confidence intervals**, absolute and relative effects, NNT.
8. **Applicability** to your patients, and **clinical significance** (a statistically significant difference may be too small to matter; a non-significant result may reflect low power).

## Trials and their limits

Controls (placebo, active, usual care), randomisation and blinding, exclusion criteria (limit generalisability), surrogate outcomes, early stopping, industry funding and conflicts of interest, the gap between efficacy (ideal conditions) and effectiveness (real world). **Pragmatic trials**, registries and large national audits (NAPs, NELA) complement RCTs.

## Systematic reviews and meta-analysis

Comprehensive searches, risk of bias assessment, heterogeneity (I^2^), fixed versus random effects, publication bias (funnel plots), and the certainty of evidence (GRADE). Pitfalls: pooling low-quality or heterogeneous studies.

## Governance and ethics

Research ethics committee approval, **informed consent** (including emergency research processes when patients lack capacity), data protection, sponsorship, Good Clinical Practice, the roles of Trust R&D and university departments, and translation of findings into practice.

## Communicating evidence

Explain risks using absolute numbers and natural frequencies (e.g. "about 1 in 100 people"), visual aids, and honest statements of uncertainty; national reports (e.g. MBRRACE for maternal outcomes) help set context.
