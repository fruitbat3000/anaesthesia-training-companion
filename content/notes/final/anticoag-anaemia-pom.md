## Patient blood management (PBM)

Three pillars: **(1) optimise the patient's own red cell mass** (detect and treat anaemia before surgery), **(2) minimise blood loss** (stop antithrombotics appropriately, surgical technique, tranexamic acid, cell salvage, point-of-care testing to guide products), **(3) optimise tolerance of anaemia** (restrictive transfusion thresholds, oxygen delivery).

## Preoperative anaemia

- Common (a third of major surgery patients) and associated with worse outcomes and more transfusion.
- **Investigate:** full blood count, ferritin, transferrin saturation, CRP, B~12~ and folate, renal function; consider GI investigation for iron deficiency.
- **Treat:** oral iron if time allows (weeks), **IV iron** if surgery is within about 6 weeks or oral iron fails; treat B~12~/folate deficiency; erythropoiesis-stimulating agents in selected cases.
- **Postoperative anaemia:** IV iron and restrictive transfusion (international consensus 2018).

## Anticoagulants and antiplatelets

- **Balance thrombosis risk** (e.g. mechanical mitral valve, recent VTE, recent stroke, AF with high CHA~2~DS~2~-VASc) against **bleeding risk** (procedure type, neuraxial block).
- **Warfarin:** stop about 5 days before; bridge with LMWH only for high thrombotic risk; check INR (≤ 1.5 for most surgery; lower for neuraxial); reverse urgently with PCC and vitamin K.
- **DOACs:** stop 1–3 days (or more for dabigatran with poor renal function and high-bleeding-risk or neuraxial procedures) before; no bridging; restart when haemostasis allows.
- **Antiplatelets:** aspirin usually continued; clopidogrel/prasugrel/ticagrelor stopped (5–7 days) if safe — never stop DAPT for recent stents without cardiology advice.
- **Neuraxial blocks:** follow the Association of Anaesthetists/OAA/RA-UK guidance on timing (see the Primary note on anticoagulants).

## Refusal of blood

Jehovah's Witnesses: discuss in detail which products and techniques are acceptable (cell salvage, albumin, clotting factor concentrates vary by individual), document an advance decision, optimise haemoglobin, plan meticulous haemostasis and early escalation. A competent adult's refusal is legally binding even if death may result; children's treatment may require legal involvement.
