## Candidate instructions

You are the senior anaesthetic resident on call. A CT2 colleague, **Dr Lewis** (a role player), tells you that during a laparoscopic appendicectomy they gave **metaraminol** instead of **ondansetron** at the end of the case (similar syringes). The patient had a transient rise in BP to 190/100 mmHg and a heart rate of 45, which resolved within 10 minutes. The patient is now awake in recovery and stable.

**Tasks:** talk to Dr Lewis, then explain what happened to the patient (the examiner).

## Scenario

Dr Lewis is upset and worried about their career. The syringes were both 10 mL, handwritten labels, drawn up at the start of the list by a different anaesthetist. The patient, **Mrs Fox**, feels well but asks why her heart was "racing and then slow" as she woke.

## Examiner prompts

(Role player cues)
- Dr Lewis: "I'm so sorry — will I be in trouble?"
- Dr Lewis: "Do we have to tell her? She's fine."
- Patient: "What exactly happened?"
- Patient: "Could it happen again? What are you doing about it?"

## Key features

- Checks patient safety first (observations, ECG, need for troponin/monitoring)
- Supports the colleague non-judgementally, acknowledges distress, and signposts support
- Explains the duty of candour and why the patient must be told
- Ensures an incident report is completed and learning is shared (not blame)
- Identifies system factors: pre-drawn unlabelled/handwritten syringes, similar syringes, handovers, fatigue
- Tells the patient honestly and clearly what happened, apologises, explains effects and monitoring, and what will change
- Documents the conversation and offers written follow-up
- Escalates to the consultant and suggests departmental learning (labels, drug trays, M&M)

## Domains assessed

Honesty and integrity, team working and support, empathy, task management.

## What good looks like

A kind, calm response to Dr Lewis ("thank you for telling me — let's make sure Mrs Fox is safe, then we'll sort this out together"), a clear and genuine apology to the patient without jargon, and a systems-based approach to learning.

## Learning points

- The duty of candour applies even when no lasting harm results.
- Most drug errors have system causes: labelling, look-alike syringes, pre-drawn drugs.
- Supporting staff after errors (second victims) is part of good leadership.
